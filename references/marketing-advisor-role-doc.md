SECTION 2: CORE RESPONSIBILITIES (6–8 key responsibilities with what, frequency, AI skill opportunity)

Plan and execute marketing campaigns – Design multi-channel campaigns (email, web, social, events) to generate leads and promote EDC solutions. Define target segments, craft messaging, coordinate creative/content, and launch via marketing automation tools. Frequency: Planning occurs quarterly with multiple campaigns running weekly. AI opportunity: Prompting for Outcomes – using Copilot to draft campaign copy or brainstorm content ideas based on prompts (e.g. generating an email template from a prompt that specifies audience and tone).
Content creation and brand messaging – Write and edit marketing content: emails, blog articles, social posts, web pages, brochures. Ensure all copy follows EDC’s brand guidelines, bilingual requirements, and legal/ethical standards. Frequency: Daily content development; major content pieces weekly or monthly. AI opportunity: Verification and Judgment – reviewing and fact-checking AI-generated text for accuracy and brand compliance before publication (e.g. verifying product details in an AI-drafted blog post).
Event marketing & lead capture – Coordinate EDC’s presence at trade shows, webinars, and partner events. Handle event logistics from a marketing side: create event pages/invitations, prepare on-site materials, set up lead capture forms (e.g. in Eloqua), and ensure data (contacts, business cards, consent) is captured properly. After events, extract and upload leads to CRM and trigger follow-ups. Frequency: ~Monthly (peak seasons for industry events; planning starts ~5–6 weeks prior to each event). AI opportunity: Data Safety and Compliance – using AI tools to help process lead data without exposing personal/contact details, and checking that all follow-up content respects consent flags and privacy guidelines.
Lead generation & nurturing – Attract and qualify new prospect leads for sales. This includes managing inbound inquiries (via EDC.ca, webinars, content downloads), outbound email marketing for prospecting, and running Account-Based Marketing (ABM) initiatives in collaboration with sales. Marketing monitors lead activity (email opens, website engagement) and applies scoring or initial qualification criteria, then routes leads to the appropriate sales team (RMs or ARMs) for follow-up. Frequency: Ongoing/daily; hundreds of leads per month (e.g. ~60% of ~600 new prospect leads per ARM annually come via Marketing campaigns). AI opportunity: Tool Fluency (M365 + Copilot) – utilizing CRM Copilot or Power BI AI to quickly analyze lead lists and engagement metrics (while understanding the CRM and analytics tools’ outputs) to refine which leads are “hot” vs. “nurture,” and using Copilot in Outlook to draft personalized outreach at scale.
Sales enablement & communications – Provide customer-focused materials and templates to client-facing teams. This includes crafting email copy, pitch deck slides, product sheets, success stories, and social media messages that RMs or partners can use for outreach. Marketing localizes and tailors these assets for different industries or customer segments. Frequency: Weekly, often on short notice when a new offer launches or a territory asks for custom collateral. AI opportunity: Prompting for Outcomes – formulating effective prompts to have Copilot generate first drafts of sales emails or LinkedIn posts in the right tone, then iterating based on the outcome.
Campaign performance analysis – Track and report on marketing results. Gather data on KPIs like email open/click rates, web traffic, lead conversion, and brand health metrics. Produce dashboards or summaries for leadership (e.g. quarterly marketing performance reviews). Use insights to adjust strategies (e.g. refining targeting or channel mix). Frequency: Monthly dashboards; deep-dive analysis quarterly. AI opportunity: Verification and Judgment – using Copilot in Excel/PowerBI to summarize data trends, but applying human judgment to verify that the AI’s analysis makes sense (e.g. spotting if an AI-generated trend insight is real or a data artifact) before advising strategy changes.
Brand and compliance oversight – Act as a guardian of EDC’s brand and ethical guidelines in all communications. Review marketing outputs for consistent tone, proper disclaimers (e.g. not implying guarantees), and legal compliance (e.g. CASL opt-out included, privacy statements). Train internal partners on do’s and don’ts (e.g. “General Advertising Rules” and intellectual property use). Frequency: Integrated into every content task (daily); formal brand review checkpoints for major campaigns. AI opportunity: Data Safety and Compliance – ensuring that any use of AI in content creation does not introduce biased or non-compliant language, and that confidential info (like client names or non-public figures) are stripped out of prompts and outputs. SECTION 3: WORKFLOW AND TOOLS
A) Workflow Snapshot:

Weekly rhythm: Mondays often involve team status meetings and reviewing campaign metrics from the previous week. Mid-week is heavy on execution – content production, scheduling emails, and cross-functional check-ins (e.g. meeting with product or segment teams about upcoming campaigns). End-of-week typically includes reporting tasks (compiling results, updating dashboards) and backlog grooming (prepping assets for next week’s launches).
Recurring rituals: Monthly marketing-performance review meeting (with Marketing leadership or segment heads) to discuss pipeline contribution, campaign outcomes, and upcoming plans. Quarterly integrated planning sessions with Sales and Sectors teams to align on priorities (e.g. Integrated Regional Planning meetings where Marketing, SIA, and MMB decide which events or campaigns to run jointly in the next quarter).
Peak periods: Campaign launch days and event weeks are high-intensity, often requiring after-hours coordination (e.g. monitoring webinar registrations up to go-live, or last-minute changes to event materials). End of quarter and end of year bring spikes in demand for marketing support (to meet targets or prep annual reports, etc.).
Most time-consuming activities: (1) Content/collateral development – writing, editing, and obtaining approvals for numerous pieces (requires coordination with Legal/Compliance and French translation, which can slow the process). (2) Data cleanup and list management – ensuring marketing contact lists are up-to-date (removing dupes, checking consent flags) and manually migrating data between systems (e.g. downloading Eloqua lists to Excel, formatting, then uploading to CRM) can consume many hours.
Most repetitive tasks: (1) Preparing standard communications – e.g. drafting similar event invite emails or product promo copy with only slight changes for different audiences (currently often copied from last version and tweaked). (2) Monthly reporting – pulling the same KPI data from multiple sources (web analytics, email platform, CRM) and pasting into PowerPoint or Excel templates for management reports. Much of this process is manual and repeated for each report cycle.

SECTION 4: DATA AND COMPLIANCE

Sensitive data handled: Marketing regularly works with personal and company-identifying data of prospects and customers – names, job titles, emails, phone numbers, and company financial profile info (especially for targeted campaigns). They also access engagement data (what content a prospect viewed or clicked) which is considered non-public behavioral data. Event marketing involves collecting business card details and confirming email consent (CASL status) for each contact – this PII must be stored and used carefully. Internally, marketers see performance data that might be sensitive (e.g. campaign ROI, brand survey results by region) and early drafts of public announcements.
Key compliance/regulations: Canada’s Anti-Spam Legislation (CASL) is a critical constraint – marketing must obtain and document consent before sending promotional emails. This means strict adherence to unsubscribe management, consent checkboxes on web forms, and keeping proof (e.g. scanned business cards with implicit consent notes). Privacy laws (e.g. the Privacy Act/PIPEDA) require that any personal information (even business contact info) is protected – marketing must ensure contact lists are stored securely and only used for intended purposes. Also, marketing communications must follow Official Languages Act requirements (all external content must be bilingual). Accessibility standards apply for digital content (e.g. following WCAG guidelines for website material).
“Safe AI use” boundaries (GenAI policy): Marketing employees are explicitly prohibited from inputting any Non-Public EDC information into unsanctioned AI tools. They must never share customer data (names, contact details, company identifiers) or internal strategy documents when using generative AI. For example, they cannot copy-paste a raw lead list or an unreleased marketing plan into ChatGPT or public Copilot Chat. According to EDC’s GenAI policy, all outputs used externally must be human-reviewed and attributed, and users should avoid prompting AI with content that includes confidential deal information, financials, or any personal data that hasn’t been anonymized. In short – no customer PII, no detailed company data, no unapproved content should be given to AI, and any AI-generated text must meet the same compliance checks as human-written text.
Data-related risks: The biggest risk is accidentally breaching customer privacy or consent – e.g. sending an email to someone who opted out, or exposing personal info in a way that violates CASL (could lead to legal fines and reputational damage). Data accuracy is also a concern: if marketing mis-segments data (e.g. a lead tagged to the wrong industry) it can lead to embarrassing mistakes in messaging. Using AI without caution introduces hallucination risk – an AI-written content piece might insert an incorrect statistic or promise, which if not caught could mislead customers or violate regulations. Reputational risk is high if marketing communications are off-brand or erroneous (the public facing nature means any error is visible). Finally, data security: marketing often possesses large contact files – a leak or sending the file to an external party by mistake would compromise thousands of contacts. The team must handle spreadsheets and exports diligently (e.g. using encrypted storage, limiting email attachments of contact lists).
SECTION 5: PAIN POINTS AND AI OPPORTUNITIES

Manual campaign workflows: A lot of marketing work is still manual or in disparate systems (e.g. building an email in Eloqua, then separately updating results in Excel). Repetitive tasks like copying content between English and French versions, or resizing the same imagery across platforms, consume time. AI opportunity: Automating these small tasks (e.g. using Copilot to instantly draft the French version once English is approved, or using image AI to auto-generate properly sized graphics) could free up hours and reduce human error.
Lead hand-off inefficiencies: After marketing generates leads (from web forms or events), the process of assigning and following up can be a bottleneck. It relies on coordination with Sales Operations and RMs, and sometimes leads “leak” (e.g. not followed up promptly). Pain point: Marketers lack visibility into lead outcomes once handed over. AI opportunity: An AI assistant could track lead progression and nudge sales if follow-ups lag, or even triage basic inquiries instantly. This would improve conversion and give marketing better feedback on lead quality.
Content volume vs. capacity: The team is under pressure to produce a high volume of tailored content (for multiple segments, products, and in both languages) without a proportional increase in headcount. Pain point: They often resort to “cut and paste” reuse, risking that content isn’t fully adapted. Where AI could help quality: AI could act as a first-draft generator – e.g. providing a baseline article or social post that the marketer then refines. This would allow the team to explore more creative options and iterations than time currently permits, potentially improving the quality and variety of content delivered.
Bottlenecks in approvals and compliance: Getting every piece of content vetted by Legal/Compliance (and translated) can slow campaigns, causing missed timely opportunities. For example, by the time a whitepaper is cleared, the market trend it addresses may have cooled. AI opportunity: Use AI to pre-check content against compliance rules or generate quicker summaries for approval (so approvers read a one-pager instead of a 10-page draft). This could accelerate the review cycle. (As of now, this is a hypothesis – such AI use would need its own approval. Hypothesis)
Data analysis and insight generation: Marketers are not data scientists, yet they are expected to glean insights from campaign metrics and customer research. Today this involves manually slicing spreadsheets and guesswork on trends. AI opportunity: Copilot’s analytical abilities could surface non-obvious patterns (e.g. “Copilot, show which customer segment engaged most with our webinar series and summarize their attributes”). This would enable more data-driven decisions on where to focus marketing efforts, something the team currently struggles to do thoroughly due to time.
If given +2 hours/week (hypothetical): Hypothesis: Many marketing staff would invest extra time in strategic planning and skill development – e.g. finally updating that segment strategy, learning a new analytics tool, or brainstorming creative campaign ideas (activities often squeezed out by urgent deliverables). Others might use it to better collaborate with sales – sitting in on sales calls or visiting customers to deepen their understanding, which in turn would improve marketing messaging. In short, freed capacity would likely go toward activities that currently feel “luxury,” like creative testing (A/B experiments they seldom have time to run) or proactive market research, which would enhance future marketing quality.
SECTION 6: AI TRAINING DESIGN SEEDS
A) Operational anchors (real tasks for AI exercises):

Drafting bilingual campaign emails: Use Copilot in Word to generate a first draft of a promotional email in English (with correct tone and key points), then have it produce a French translation – testing strategic_prompting (for style/tone control) and critical_eval (to ensure the output in both languages is accurate and on-brand).
Event lead follow-up prep: After a trade show, ask Copilot (integrated with CRM data) to summarize 50 new leads’ profiles and engagement history into a follow-up priority list – then refine it manually. This task tests data_decision skills (interpreting AI-curated lead insights) and responsible_ai judgment (only using anonymized data in the AI prompt).
Social media post creation: Prompt an AI assistant to draft a LinkedIn post announcing a client success story, including a catchy intro and key results. The marketer then edits the post to add the client’s name and ensure it meets compliance (no exaggerations) – exercising augmented_comm (collaborative content creation) and verification of facts.
Monthly metrics review: Input last month’s email campaign stats into Excel and have Copilot generate a bullet list of insights (e.g. which subject line performed best, which region engaged the most), then double-check those claims against the raw data. This trains critical_eval (don’t blindly trust AI analysis) while improving tool_fluency with Excel Copilot.
ABM account research with AI: Using an AI tool to gather public intel on a target company (size, recent news, industry trends) and compile talking points for a meeting. The marketer must integrate this with internal knowledge (from CRM notes) that AI can’t access. This scenario builds relationship_intel (combining AI research with human context) and judgment in filtering what’s relevant.
Brand tone check: Take a piece of content drafted by an AI (e.g. a blog intro) and compare it to EDC’s style guidelines using an AI plugin or prompt. The learner adjusts phrasing and terminology where the AI missed the mark (e.g. replacing overly promotional language with a more consultative tone). This anchor focuses on verification & judgment and reinforces responsible_ai use (ensuring AI outputs meet corporate values).

C) Domain-to-workflow map:

Domain	Highest-Risk / Highest-Value Moments for This Role
Responsible AI	– Using generative AI to process contact lists or CRM data (risk of leaking personal or non-public info if not handled carefully). 
 – Drafting external content with AI assistance under tight deadlines (must ensure no confidential or misleading info is output in the rush to publish).
Strategic Prompting	– Content creation tasks like writing campaign emails or social posts (output quality depends on how well the prompt conveys audience, tone, and key points). 
 – Data analysis queries in Copilot (asking the right question of marketing data to get a useful insight, e.g. prompting “compare engagement by segment” vs a vague “tell me what’s interesting”).
Critical Evaluation	– Reviewing AI-generated copy for a product or policy detail (the marketer must spot if the AI has introduced an incorrect claim or inappropriate wording). 
 – Interpreting AI analytics: when Copilot or Power BI auto-summarizes campaign results, marketers need to critically assess whether the conclusion is supported by the data (e.g. AI says “web traffic up 50%” – is that real or a glitch?).
Relationship Intelligence	– Account-specific marketing (ABM): customizing messaging for key clients requires combining AI outputs with intimate knowledge of the client’s business and the RM’s relationship, something AI alone lacks. 
 – Cross-team collaboration: understanding internal stakeholders (sales, underwriters) – e.g. knowing an RM’s communication style or a product team’s concerns – ensures that AI-crafted drafts still fit the stakeholder context when shared for input.
Data-Driven Decision Making	– Lead scoring/prioritization: deciding which leads or prospects to pursue from a large list (AI can surface patterns, but the marketer decides based on business fit and segment strategy). 
 – Channel optimization: choosing where to allocate budget (email vs events vs ads) by examining performance data – AI might highlight a trend, but the marketer must decide if it’s noise or a true signal and act accordingly.
Augmented Communication	– High-stakes communications: e.g. drafting CEO messages to customers or crisis communications – AI can help with structure and grammar, but the marketer must heavily curate tone and content (the human touch is crucial to avoid PR issues). 
 – Volume communications: e.g. personalization at scale – using AI to help tailor 100+ outreach emails while maintaining quality. The marketer’s role is to oversee and tweak each AI-personalized note so it remains sincere and relevant.
