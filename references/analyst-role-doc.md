# Role Intelligence Profile: **Analysts (Non-IT) – Export Development Canada (EDC)**

## SECTION 1: ROLE FUNDAMENTALS

*   **role\_prefix:** **an** (analyst)
*   **Official Titles & Variants:** *Analyst* (general title used in multiple teams); **Information Analyst** (with levels IA-15 through IA-18); **Risk Analyst** (e.g. within Risk Assessment & Portfolio Management); **Financial/FP\&A Analyst** and **Lead Analyst, FP\&A** (in Enterprise Analytics & Forecasting); **Customer Experience Analyst** (in customer insight teams); **Business Development Analyst** (referred to as *BD analyst*, supporting Relationship Managers). *(Non-IT Analysts span various business units and may have specific designations like “Senior Analyst” or “Principal Analyst” at higher levels.)*
*   **Departments/Business Units:** Analysts (non-IT) are distributed across EDC’s **business and corporate teams**. Key areas include: **Company Information Management (CIM)** within Business Services (Information Analysts managing customer data); **Risk Management/Credit Insurance** (Risk Analysts supporting underwriting and portfolio management); **Enterprise Analytics & Forecasting (EAF)** under Finance (FP\&A analysts delivering financial and portfolio analytics); **Business Development** (analysts supporting sales teams with client research and analysis); and other specialized units like **Economics** or **Market Research** (quantitative analysts handling economic and market data).
*   **Typical Reporting Lines:** Non-IT Analysts report to functional managers or directors in their respective domains. *For example:* Information Analysts report to CIM Managers (with a “Principal” overseeing data quality); Risk Analysts often report to **Risk Management** team leads or directors (e.g. **RAPM Managers** in credit insurance); FP\&A Analysts report through the **Enterprise Analytics & Forecasting** hierarchy up to Finance leadership (e.g. Director of EAF and CFO); Analysts supporting Business Development or other lines may report to a **Regional Director** or **Team Leader** in those units.
*   **Approximate Headcount:** **Information Analysts – 21** in the CIM team (as of mid-2024). **Credit/Financial Analysts – \~15** for corporate credit risk (plus **5–10** for SME credit) in underwriting and risk teams. **FP\&A/Analytics Analysts – not found** (multiple “Analyst” and “Lead Analyst” roles exist in EAF, but exact count is not available – *likely dozens*).
*   **Seniority Levels:** **Information Analysts** have multiple levels (e.g. **IA-15 through IA-18**) indicating a progression from junior to senior analyst within CIM. **FP\&A Analysts** have **Analyst** and **Lead Analyst** tiers, and other teams may use similar “Senior” or “Principal Analyst” designations. *In general, non-IT analysts range from entry-level analysts up to principal/lead analyst roles, with increasing responsibility for complex analysis and advisory contributions.*

## SECTION 2: CORE RESPONSIBILITIES (Key Activities)

**Analysts (Non-IT) at EDC perform a wide array of analytical and data-related tasks across the organization. Core responsibilities typically include:**

*   **New Company Onboarding & Data Verification:** **Validating and creating customer/counterparty records** in EDC’s CRM (**C3** – “Connect, Communicate, Collaborate”) by researching official sources (government registries, company databases like Mergent/Orbis/Capital IQ). **Frequency:** Daily, as new prospects register on EDC’s portals or sales teams request new company profiles. **Inputs:** Registration info from companies (e.g. EDC.ca sign-ups) or internal requests via ServiceNow (analyst ticketing system). Analysts confirm legal name, status, and gather key data (addresses, industry codes, ownership) from trusted sources. **Outputs:** A **standardized company profile** in C3 with accurate data (legal name, address, industry, ownership, etc.), often accompanied by attached documents (e.g. registry certificates, financial statements) stored in the CRM or SharePoint. This **enriched profile** is **consumed by Relationship Managers, underwriters, and all lines of business** as the single source of truth for customer information. **System-of-Record:** *C3 (Dynamics CRM)* is the primary repository for customer data. **AI Opportunity:** *Tool Fluency (M365 + Copilot)* – e.g. using Copilot to **extract and summarize data from websites or documents** to speed up populating CRM fields, while still requiring verification for accuracy.

*   **Data Quality Maintenance & Duplicate Resolution:** **Monitoring and cleaning up customer data** to ensure completeness, accuracy, and uniqueness of records. Analysts **continuously refresh key data fields** (e.g. updated addresses, status changes, corporate family links) and use de-duplication tools to identify and merge duplicate entries. **Frequency:** Ongoing weekly/daily task, with dedicated data quality sprints as needed. **Inputs:** System alerts (e.g. potential duplicate company names), updates from customers or account managers, external data feeds (e.g. bankruptcy notices, name changes). **Outputs:** **Corrected and updated records** in the CRM, along with internal communications or notes to stakeholders if significant changes occur (e.g. notifying account teams of a name change or data discrepancy). **System-of-Record:** *C3 CRM* (with possible use of **ServiceNow’s Analyst Portal** to track and manage data correction cases). **AI Opportunity:** *Data Safety and Compliance* – ensuring that **automated tools for data cleaning adhere to EDC’s data standards**, flag uncertainties for human review, and do not inadvertently merge or delete critical data without validation.

*   **Credit Risk Analysis for Transactions (Underwriting Support):** **Analyzing financial statements and credit information of buyers/borrowers** to assess risk for loans, guarantees, or insurance cover requests. Analysts (often titled **Risk Analysts** or **Credit Analysts**) work with Underwriters on new credit limit applications and renewals. They scrutinize financial reports, credit agency reports, industry data, and internal exposure records. **Frequency:** Daily, as new credit requests or renewals come in; volume can be high (e.g. \~1,000 corporate credit assessments per year, with peak volumes in March–June when annual financials are released). **Inputs:** Borrower financial statements (often obtained via the line of business or Info Sourcing team); credit bureau reports and bank references; internal exposure data from systems like *CIS* (Credit Insurance System) or *LOS* (loan origination system “Clarity”); country and industry risk ratings from EDC’s economists or external sources. **Outputs:** **Risk assessments and recommendations** – e.g., a written *Buyer Assessment* or credit memo documenting financial ratios, risk ratings, and recommended credit limits. These are reviewed by Underwriters and, if above certain thresholds, by Risk Management Office (RMO) or credit committees for decision. **Systems-of-Record:** *CIS* (for credit insurance buyer risk info and decisions); *FACT* (EDC’s internal financial analysis & credit rating workflow tool); and soon *Moody’s CreditLens* (replacing FACT for credit risk and financial spreading). **AI Opportunity:** *Verification and Judgment* – using AI to rapidly analyze financial statements or economic conditions **with human oversight**. For instance, EDC is piloting **“QuiQspread” AI** to auto-extract and populate financial spreads, but analysts must validate calculations and assumptions made by the AI. Analysts also apply judgment to ensure the **AI’s risk outputs align with known business realities**.

*   **Portfolio Monitoring & Reviews (Risk Management):** **Tracking the performance and risk of existing obligors or insured buyers** over time. Analysts perform **scheduled reviews (e.g. “SPEC reviews”)** on portfolios of buyers or counterparts, focusing on changes in creditworthiness or risk ratings. They also monitor covenant compliance for loan portfolios (often handled by specialized *Covenant Analysts* on the same team as financial spreaders). **Frequency:** *Periodic* – e.g., annual or semi-annual reviews on significant exposures, and **ongoing** covenant checks when borrowers submit financial results (quarterly or annually, per loan agreements). **Inputs:** Internal systems (CIS/FACT) with exposure data and ticklers for review dates; updated financial statements; news or external rating changes; covenants from loan agreements (monitored via a *covenant tracking sheet or system*). **Outputs:** **Review reports or updates to risk ratings and renewal decisions**. Analysts document any change in risk level, update validity dates or exposure limits in the system, and flag issues (e.g. covenant breaches or deteriorating financials) to management. Results may trigger follow-up actions (e.g. closer monitoring or escalation to credit committees for high-risk cases). **System-of-Record:** *CIS* and *FACT* contain the official record of buyer reviews, risk ratings, and covenants. **AI Opportunity:** *Data Safety and Compliance* – AI could assist by scanning news and financial data for early warning signals, but analysts must apply judgment to ensure **no false alarms or missed risks**, and to maintain compliance with credit policies and privacy (e.g. avoid over-reliance on AI for critical credit decisions).

*   **Financial Planning & Analysis (FP\&A):** **Analyzing EDC’s financial and operational performance** and supporting corporate planning. Analysts in **Enterprise Analytics & Forecasting (EAF)** prepare monthly and quarterly reports on key financial metrics, portfolio performance, and business drivers for senior management. They also contribute to **annual budgeting and forecasting processes**. **Frequency:** *Monthly* (performance dashboards and variance analysis), *quarterly* (forecasts and board reports), and *annual* (budget cycle). **Inputs:** Data from EDC’s financial systems and data warehouse (revenues, expenses, capital, portfolio metrics) and business line reports; data from Power BI dashboards and Excel models; external economic indicators as needed. **Outputs:** **Management reports and dashboards** – e.g., scorecards showing business volumes, financial results vs targets, customer metrics (NPS, retention, etc.). Typically delivered as **PowerPoint decks or Power BI interactive reports** to executives and business unit leaders. Also, **analysis memos** explaining trends, variances, and recommendations for action (used by the CFO, VPs, and strategy teams). **Systems-of-Record:** *Power BI and internal data warehouses* for storing and presenting metrics; *Excel models* for detailed analysis; *SharePoint/OneDrive* for distributing reports. **AI Opportunity:** *Prompting for Outcomes* – e.g., using **Copilot in Excel or Power BI** to quickly generate variance explanations or projections by querying large datasets in plain language, then refining prompts to get scenario analyses. (Analysts would benefit from faster iteration in forecasting “what-if” scenarios, while still applying their expertise to vet the AI’s suggestions.)

*   **Business & Market Research Support:** **Conducting specialized analyses to support business decisions or strategy.** Some analysts focus on market intelligence, economic research, or product analytics. They might analyze trade data, economic trends, or customer survey results to inform EDC’s strategies. **Frequency:** *Project-based and periodic* – e.g., quarterly market reports, ad-hoc research projects for strategic initiatives, or as needed for new product development. **Inputs:** Internal data (e.g. EDC’s portfolio data, customer surveys with thousands of responses); external data sources (macroeconomic databases like Haver Analytics or Oxford Economics, industry reports, competitor info). **Outputs:** **Research briefs, whitepapers or presentations** summarizing findings and insights (e.g., a “market scan” report for a technology trend or an economic outlook for a region). These are often used by strategy teams, product managers, or senior leadership for decision-making. **System-of-Record:** *Shared drives or EDC’s knowledge library* (e.g., stored on SharePoint or as published insight reports). **AI Opportunity:** *Tool Fluency / Verification and Judgment* – *e.g.*, employing an **AI research agent** to gather information from internal and external document repositories and draft a market scan, while the analyst reviews the output for accuracy and context. The analyst’s role is critical in ensuring the AI’s findings are corroborated and relevant, applying professional skepticism before use.

*   **Internal Advisory & Decision Support:** **Providing data-driven recommendations to stakeholders.** Analysts frequently act as **internal consultants**, translating data into insights for other teams. Examples include **advising account managers on cross-selling opportunities** (e.g., analyzing a client portfolio to suggest upsell opportunities), or advising risk management on portfolio concentration limits with analytics. **Frequency:** *Regularly*, often as part of cross-functional projects or strategy updates. **Inputs:** A combination of internal performance data, customer information from CRM, and external benchmarks or best practices. **Outputs:** **Slide decks, briefing notes, or tailored dashboards** delivered to the requesting team (e.g., a set of slides for a lending strategy workshop, or a briefing note on a client’s risk profile for a senior account review). **Systems:** *Microsoft PowerPoint/Word* for producing the advice in a consumable format; underlying data in *Excel/Power BI/CRM*. **AI Opportunity:** *Prompting for Outcomes* – Analysts can harness AI (like Copilot’s **“Ask me anything” in Power BI or Excel**) to query data and generate initial insights or visualizations, accelerating the advisory process. However, they must use their judgment to refine the AI’s outputs into credible recommendations. *(If no clear AI application, mark “not applicable.”)*

*   **Process Improvement & Data Governance Participation:** Many analysts contribute to **improving processes and data governance standards** in their domains. For instance, they might help define requirements for system changes (like the transition from FACT to CreditLens for credit analysis), or participate in data stewardship and governance forums to improve data quality (as “everyone is a data steward” per EDC’s Data Governance playbook). **Frequency:** *Periodic* – as part of projects or working groups. **Inputs:** pain points from daily work (manual steps, system limitations) and data quality issues observed. **Outputs:** **Feedback, business requirements, or updated guidelines** (e.g., contributing to a new credit process documentation or to the Responsible AI policy development as subject matter experts). **Systems:** *SharePoint/Teams* for collaboration; project management tools for tracking improvements. **AI Opportunity:** *Tool Fluency & Data Safety* – familiarity with AI tools themselves is becoming a responsibility (e.g., learning how to use new AI-driven analytics tools safely). Also, analysts are often pilot users of new AI solutions, helping to craft guardrails (like the **Analyst Agent guardrails** document created by EDC’s AI Centre of Enablement with input from analysts). *This develops their skill in “data safety and compliance” as they help design processes that incorporate AI responsibly.*

*(Additional responsibilities and nuances may exist for specific Analyst roles. The above captures common core activities across non-IT analyst positions at EDC.)*

## SECTION 3: DAILY AND WEEKLY WORKFLOWS

*   **Typical Monday Morning:** Analysts generally start the week by **reviewing new requests and pending tasks**. For instance, a **CIM Information Analyst** checks the ServiceNow **Analyst Portal** for any new company onboarding cases that came in over the weekend, prioritizing any high-priority or near-SLA-breach cases. A **Risk/Credit Analyst** might begin Monday by reading through emails and credit system queues for any new loan or insurance credit applications submitted late Friday, triaging them by urgency or approaching deadlines. FP\&A Analysts often use Monday mornings to gather the latest data (e.g. last week’s sales figures or month-to-date financials) and update tracking spreadsheets or dashboards for the week’s kick-off meetings. In all cases, Monday is about **catching up on communications, organizing tasks, and setting priorities** for the week ahead.

*   **Weekly Patterns and Cadence:** Many analysts have **cyclical peaks** aligned with business rhythms. For example, **credit risk analysts** in corporate lending experience heavy workload **in Q1–Q2 (March to June)** when annual financial statements arrive and \~1,000 Probability-of-Default reviews are conducted (the busiest period for spreading and analyzing financials). **FP\&A analysts** follow the financial cycle: early in the month they compile results and close the books, mid-month they prepare management reports, and end-of-quarter brings intensified activity for forecasts and Board reports. **Analysts in sales support roles** (BD analysts) often have activity spikes around quarterly business reviews or major client engagements, aligning analyses with sales team targets and campaigns. Throughout each week, analysts balance **ongoing tasks (data updates, routine reports)** with **ad-hoc requests** (unplanned queries from managers or urgent analysis for deals/opportunities).

*   **Recurring Meetings & Rituals:** Non-IT analysts participate in a variety of scheduled meetings:
    *   **Team Huddles/Check-ins:** Each analyst team (CIM, risk, FP\&A, etc.) holds regular meetings (often weekly). For instance, Information Analysts might have a weekly team meeting to discuss data quality issues or process improvements. Risk Analyst teams may hold *portfolio review meetings* or pipeline meetings with Underwriters to go over upcoming credit decisions.
    *   **Cross-Functional Meetings:** Analysts frequently join meetings with other teams as **subject matter experts**. A risk analyst may attend a **Credit Committee or RMO (Risk Management Office) meeting** to present a high-value deal’s risk assessment (though senior underwriters typically lead the meeting). FP\&A analysts often attend **monthly business unit performance reviews** or forecast meetings, where they present financial results or analysis to business leaders. They may not lead these meetings but are expected to explain the numbers and answer questions from executives.
    *   **Project & Improvement Sessions:** Analysts are involved in projects and may attend design workshops or governance meetings. Example: a data analyst or business analyst might join a **project working group meeting** for the CreditLens implementation to provide input on data migration or requirement gaps. Similarly, analysts engaged in process improvement (e.g., within the Process Performance Improvement team) might have bi-weekly scrums to discuss automation or data governance initiatives.

*   **Most Time-Consuming Activities:** A significant portion of an analyst’s week is spent on **data gathering and preparation**. This includes pulling data from systems, cleaning it, and reconciling discrepancies. For risk and financial analysts, **reading and extracting information from documents** (financial statements, credit reports, policy documents) and then populating that data into analysis templates or systems (often manually) can be very time-intensive. **Writing reports or analyses** is another major time sink – whether it’s drafting a detailed credit review, preparing a multi-slide performance deck, or documenting assumptions in a model, the process of articulating insights in a clear format requires substantial effort. **Quality checking and validation** of data and results also consume time; analysts double-check calculations, review peer inputs, and ensure consistency across documents (for example, verifying that a figure in an Excel model matches what’s in a PowerPoint chart).

*   **Most Repetitive Tasks:** Many analysts face repetitive manual processes. **Information Analysts** often perform repetitive copy-paste of data from external sources into the CRM, and repeatedly search multiple databases for each new company – following a standard research checklist each time. **Credit analysts** repetitively input financial statement line items into spreading tools and then into credit models (hence EDC’s interest in automating spreading with AI). They also draft many variants of credit memos which follow similar structures (executive summary, financial analysis, risk rating rationale, etc.). **FP\&A analysts** create recurring reports on a monthly/quarterly cycle, often reusing last period’s template and updating figures – this involves a lot of copying figures from one system to another (e.g. from data extracts to PowerPoint) and can become rote. Across roles, **report generation and data entry** are common repetitive duties.

*   **Low-Value / Administrative Work:** Non-IT analysts strive to spend time on analysis and insight, but they inevitably have **administrative burdens**:

    *   **Data re-entry and manipulation:** Because some systems are not fully integrated, analysts manually transfer data between systems. For example, prior to system improvements, credit analysts had to manually request financial statements from account managers and key in the data into analysis tools. Similarly, analysts have had to move large datasets (like survey results) into Excel and then into Python for analysis due to lack of a proper database solution, a highly inefficient workaround.
    *   **Approval and documentation workflows:** Risk analysts often must navigate multi-step approval processes (e.g., preparing documents for RMO or Board approval) that involve formatting reports, emailing for approvals, and uploading final documents to archives. These process steps, while necessary for governance, are time-consuming overhead.
    *   **Status tracking and follow-ups:** Analysts frequently act as coordinators to chase information or status updates – e.g., following up with a Relationship Manager for a missing document, or reminding a business unit to submit their forecast. This coordination work, often done via email or Teams chats, can feel like a distraction from “real” analysis.

    *These low-value tasks exist partly due to system limitations (legacy systems requiring manual intervention) and the need for risk controls (e.g., mandated documentation and approvals). Analysts often note these pain points and are involved in initiatives to automate or streamline such work where possible.*

## SECTION 4: TOOLS AND SYSTEMS (COMPREHENSIVE)

**Non-IT Analysts at EDC rely on a broad toolset combining standard corporate applications, specialized financial systems, and data sources:**

*   **Microsoft 365 Tools:** All analysts use core M365 applications for daily work. Key tools include **Outlook** (for email communications and calendar scheduling), **Microsoft Teams** (for internal chat, calls, and team channels, often used to discuss analysis or share quick updates), **Excel** (extensively used for data analysis, financial modeling, and managing datasets across all analyst roles), **PowerPoint** (to create presentations of analysis results for management or clients), and **Word** (to write formal reports or memos such as credit assessments). **OneDrive/SharePoint** is used for document storage and collaboration – e.g., analysts store and share their reports on SharePoint team sites (like the ARB site mentioned for Architecture decisions). **Specific usage scenarios & artifacts:** Financial analysts build multi-sheet Excel models (budgets, valuation models, credit ratio calculations) and use PowerPoint to distill findings for executives. Risk analysts draft credit memos in Word and may use Excel for covenant monitoring schedules. An FP\&A analyst might use a combination of Excel and Power BI to generate dynamic dashboards, then send out a PDF or PPT summary to stakeholders. **Pain Points:** Heavy use of spreadsheets can lead to version control issues and manual effort in consolidation. Analysts sometimes struggle with linking data between Excel, PowerPoint, and Power BI, and historically have spent time copying updates between these tools (an area where integration with Copilot is expected to help).

*   **Customer Relationship Management (CRM) Systems:** EDC’s main CRM is **“C3”**, which stands for Connect, Communicate, Collaborate. **Usage:** Information Analysts in the CIM team are primary users – they create and maintain company records in C3, updating fields like legal names, addresses, industry codes, and linking related entities (parent/subsidiary relationships). Relationship Managers and other front-line staff also use C3 to view and update client interactions, but analysts ensure the data is accurate and standardized. **Artifacts:** Each company profile in C3 may have attachments (incorporation documents, financial statements) uploaded by analysts as part of due diligence. **Pain Points:** Prior to recent improvements, company data was scattered or duplicated across units; centralizing in C3 has helped but requires ongoing vigilance to manage duplicates and data quality. Analysts sometimes run into system limitations, such as rigid data fields that don’t capture all nuances, requiring workarounds or comments to record extra information.

*   **Credit Risk & Underwriting Systems:** Several specialized systems support credit analysis and underwriting:
    *   **CIS (Credit Information System):** Legacy system used in Credit Insurance for managing buyer limits and credit decisions. Risk Analysts and Underwriters input buyer credit assessments here and trigger application/approval tasks. CIS holds the *buyer file* and record of approvals/declines and is considered the system of record for credit insurance decisions.
    *   **FACT:** An internal workflow tool for **financial spreading and risk rating** (Probability of Default *PD* and Loss Given Default *LGD* scoring) used in the lending side. Credit analysts (a.k.a. *spreaders* or Financial Standards Managers) use FACT to input financials and generate Risk Assessment Profiles (RAP documents) for each credit deal. FACT notifies participants at various stages (underwriter, approvers, asset managers) and archives snapshots of analyses. **Pain Points:** FACT requires manual data entry of financials (from statements) and had limited integration with other systems (leading to duplicate entry in loan systems). EDC is in the process of replacing/enhancing this with modern solutions (e.g., CreditLens).
    *   **Loan Origination System (LOS) – “Clarity”:** Used by the underwriting teams for structuring and approving loans. LOS is integrated with FACT to initiate credit workflows for Project Finance deals and to ultimately book approved loans. **Pain Points:** Historically, not all risk assessment data flowed seamlessly between systems. For example, sovereign and commercial risk ratings in FACT had to be manually reconciled with LOS entries, and certain processes (like small business loan ratings) involved manual “scratchpad” calculations outside of the main workflow.
    *   **Moody’s CreditLens:** A new **commercial off-the-shelf credit assessment platform** being implemented to modernize credit analysis (project ongoing in 2025). It will replace FACT for financial spreading and integrate with EDC’s systems. As part of this, analysts are helping configure the system, plan data migration from FACT, and train an AI tool (*QuiQspread*) to auto-read financial statements. **Pain Points Addressed:** CreditLens aims to reduce manual data entry and provide better integration. However, during implementation, analysts must run parallel processes and carefully validate outputs from the new AI-driven spread tool against their manual spreads for consistency.
    *   **Covenant Tracking:** Covenants are managed through FACT or a dedicated tracker. The **“Covenants Desk”** (4–5 analysts co-located with financial spreaders) uses FACT’s covenant module to input financial covenant definitions and test results. **Pain Points:** Some covenant testing is manual, and assignment of covenant cases within the team needs clarity. Also, retrieving all necessary data (loan agreements, financial results) requires coordination with Loan Operations or the clients.

*   **Screening & Compliance Tools:** Analysts, especially in credit/risk roles, use systems for due diligence and compliance checks:
    *   **Finscan** and **Gemini**: Tools used for screening counterparties against sanctions, watchlists, and EDC’s internal criteria (e.g. checks for financial crimes risk, politically exposed persons, etc.). These are integrated into onboarding and credit processes (e.g., automatically triggered in CRM during customer onboarding). Analysts in risk roles review the outputs of these screenings and may need to coordinate with Compliance if there are hits. **Pain Point:** False positives can create extra work (needing analysts to manually clear names that aren’t true matches), but these tools are essential for regulatory compliance.

*   **Data Analysis & Reporting Tools:** For analysts in roles involving heavy data analytics (FP\&A, economics, portfolio analytics):
    *   **Power BI:** Widely used for creating dashboards and reports to visualize data for management. For example, FP\&A analysts in EAF maintain **PowerBI dashboards and scorecards** that track pipeline, portfolio, and financial metrics for different segments. Sales analytics teams also use dashboards (e.g., the Mid-Market Performance Dashboard) to track RMs’ progress on KPIs like new customer acquisition and NPS. **Pain Points:** While powerful, building complex dashboards requires significant data wrangling. Some analysts have faced challenges with data access for PowerBI – e.g., economic analysts lacked a suitable database and had to pull data from a network drive, limiting their ability to use BI tools efficiently.
    *   **Data Science Tools (Python/Databricks):** In specialized analysis roles, some analysts use Python scripting and Jupyter notebooks or Databricks to manipulate large datasets. For instance, economists and survey analysts export survey data to CSV/Excel and then use Python or **Databricks** to clean and analyze it. **Pain Points:** Lack of centralized data storage and computational resources meant that analysts have had to rely on local files (e.g., *CSV files on a shared “W drive”*) and personal tools, which is inefficient and poses data management challenges. Efforts are underway to improve data platforms (e.g., EDC’s EDP – Enterprise Data Platform – for controlled data access).
    *   **Statistical & Database Tools:** Some analysts utilize tools like **Haver Analytics** or **Bloomberg/Thomson Reuters** for economic and market data (though a gap was noted in support for certain tools like Haver/Oxford Economics, leading to workarounds). Additionally, teams may use SQL databases or data warehouses for retrieving large data sets (with IT or data engineering support).

*   **ServiceNow (Analyst Portal):** The **EDC ServiceNow platform** is used to manage internal service requests. The **Information Analysts’ work is tracked via a ServiceNow “Information Analyst Portal,”** where new company data requests are logged and assigned. Analysts can search and update cases, view high-priority or overdue items, and manage their queue in this system. Other teams (e.g., IT support) use ServiceNow as well, but for Info Analysts it is a key workflow tool integrating with their daily tasks. **Pain Points:** The interface is improving, but some functions (like certain sections “Inbox” or dashboards) are still in development or underutilized. Analysts must juggle between ServiceNow and C3 CRM when completing an onboarding request.

*   **External Research Databases:** To supplement internal systems, analysts rely on external information sources:
    *   **Corporate Data Services:** **Mergent, Orbis, S\&P Capital IQ** – subscription databases providing corporate profiles, financials, and credit scores, used by Information Analysts to populate company data and by risk analysts for credit research.
    *   **Government Registries:** Federal/provincial corporate registry websites for verifying legal status, registration numbers, and directorship of companies (critical for onboarding and due diligence).
    *   **Market & Economic Data Sources:** e.g., **Haver Analytics**, **Oxford Economics**, and industry reports for macroeconomic indicators and forecasts (used by Economics and Strategy analysts).
    *   **News and Media:** Analysts also monitor news feeds and media for relevant information (e.g., an adverse news about a counterparty’s bankruptcy or a market development). EDC’s External Communications team monitors media and provides alerts on sensitive matters, which analysts may use as input for risk or market analysis.
    *   **Pain Points:** Some external tools are not fully integrated (analysts noted the lack of direct support for certain data services, requiring manual data handling). Additionally, using multiple external sources means analysts have to cross-verify and sometimes reconcile conflicting information.

*In summary, EDC’s non-IT analysts are “power users” of information systems: they must be adept at extracting and combining data from numerous sources. While corporate tools like M365, CRM, and Power BI are common across roles, many analysts also navigate legacy systems (CIS, FACT) and evolving new platforms (CreditLens), often developing workarounds to bridge gaps. A key part of their job is not only using these tools, but also continuously improving how data flows through them (which is why analysts are involved in tool upgrades and data governance efforts).*

## SECTION 5: DOCUMENTS AND CONTENT

**A) Content Analysts CREATE regularly:**

*   **Customer/Counterparty Profiles (CRM Data Entries):** *Created by Information Analysts.* **Type:** Structured data records (with attached documents as needed). **Audience:** Internal stakeholders (sales, credit, legal, compliance – anyone who needs accurate customer information) use these records. **Length/Format:** Not a traditional text document – it’s a set of data fields and file attachments in the C3 system. **Frequency:** Daily; each new company or significant update is a “record created/updated.” **Source-of-Truth Location:** **C3 CRM system** (the record itself is the source of truth for customer data). (E.g., an Information Analyst may create 5–10 new company profiles in a busy week, each entered and saved directly in C3.)

*   **Credit Risk Assessment Memos (“Buyer Assessments” / Credit Memos):** *Created by Risk Analysts (Credit, Underwriting support).* **Type:** A written analysis of a counterparty’s creditworthiness, often following a standard template covering financial analysis, risk rating, and recommendations. **Audience:** Internal credit decision-makers – e.g., underwriters, credit managers, Risk Management Office, and sometimes credit committee or the Board for large exposures. **Length/Format:** Typically a few pages (around 2–5 pages) in **Word** or as structured fields in an internal system; may include tables of financial ratios and narrative comments. **Frequency:** Frequently – each new credit application or annual review triggers one. An active risk analyst may produce multiple per week, depending on deal flow. **Source-of-Truth Location:** The official record is often stored in the credit system (**CIS** for insurance, or **FACT/CreditLens** for loans) as part of the transaction’s file. In some cases, the memo might be uploaded to a SharePoint or Documentum repository linked to the deal.

*   **Financial & Performance Reports/Decks:** *Created by FP\&A/Analytics Analysts.* **Type:** Management reports (presentations or documents) summarizing financial results, forecasts, or business metrics. **Audience:** EDC executives, business line leaders, sometimes the Board of Directors (via CFO). **Typical Format & Length:** **PowerPoint decks** (often \~10–20 slides for monthly/quarterly reviews) or **Excel/PDF reports** with tables and charts. These include key performance indicators, trend analysis, and narrative commentary. **Frequency:** Monthly (e.g., a **monthly performance dashboard** for each business area), Quarterly (e.g., a financial results package, perhaps aligned with external reporting timelines), and Annually (the budget plan document). **Source-of-Truth Location:** Many of these reports are produced in Power BI or Excel and then distributed via email or stored on **SharePoint** (e.g., a “Performance Reports” library) for version control. The underlying data resides in enterprise data warehouses and Power BI service.

*   **Client Briefing Notes & Proposals:** *Potentially created by analysts supporting Business Development or Account Management.* **Type:** Concise briefing documents for customer meetings (often Word docs or PPT slides). **Audience:** Relationship Managers and senior leaders engaging with a client. **Format:** 1–3 page briefs or a small slide deck summarizing the client’s profile, EDC’s exposure, opportunities and risks, recent news, and proposed solutions. **Frequency:** Before significant client calls or when strategizing on key accounts (could be a few per quarter for each BD analyst). **Source-of-Truth:** Typically saved on SharePoint team sites or in CRM as an attachment to the customer record.

*   **Ad-hoc Analysis Memos:** *Created by various analysts upon request.* **Type:** Short write-ups or spreadsheets answering a specific question. For example, an analyst might prepare a **one-page memo** on “Exposure to \[Industry X] across our portfolio” for a strategy discussion, or a **spreadsheet model** projecting the financial impact of a new pricing policy. **Audience:** Internal requester (could be anyone from a product manager to the CEO, depending on the nature of the question). **Format:** Varies – often an email or Word document with key points, possibly accompanied by data in Excel. **Frequency:** Irregular, but commonly several times a month as new questions arise. **Source-of-Truth:** These might be emailed or shared via Teams; if formal, they might be stored in relevant SharePoint folders (e.g., under a project or initiative).

**B) Content Analysts RECEIVE/READ/ANALYZE regularly:**

*   **External Company Information Documents:** *Relevant to Information and Risk Analysts.* **What & Source:** Official **company registry filings** (articles of incorporation, certificates of status), **financial statements and annual reports** from clients or credit applicants, and **bank or trade references**. These often come via the company itself or through EDC’s Info Sourcing team (which the analysts can task to obtain bank reports, credit reports, etc.). **What they look for:** Confirmation of identity and legal status (for onboarding), and in financials: key figures like revenue, profitability, leverage, trends. **Actions triggered:** Use this info to populate CRM records or to perform credit ratio analysis. If something is missing or concerning (e.g., financial statements not provided or showing red flags), analysts might request additional info or flag the issue to underwriters/compliance.

*   **Internal Systems Data & Reports:** *All analysts.* **What & Source:** Data retrieved from internal systems – e.g., *CRM data extracts, risk system reports, financial system data*. A FP\&A analyst, for example, will pull data from the data warehouse or Hyperion (if used) at month-end; a risk analyst will read exposure reports from CIS or risk dashboards; an information analyst might review an automated duplicate report highlighting potential duplicate companies. **What they look for:** Relevant metrics and anomalies. FP\&A looks for budget vs actual variances, trends in product volume; risk analysts look for changes in exposure or overdue accounts; information analysts scan for data inconsistencies. **Actions:** Adjust their analysis models, update records, or alert business owners. For instance, if an FP\&A analyst sees a big variance in loan disbursements, they may reach out to the lending team for an explanation and then incorporate that into their report commentary.

*   **Policies, Guidelines, and Procedures:** Analysts must stay aware of internal policies that govern their work. For example, they refer to the **Credit Insurance Delegation of Authority (DOA) Standard** and **RMO Process guidelines** when making credit recommendations. They also consult **Data Governance standards and the Code of Conduct** for handling sensitive information, as well as the **Responsible Use of AI Policy** when considering AI tools (see Section 7). **What they look for:** The rules and limits that apply to their analysis (e.g., when a risk decision needs higher approval, how to classify confidential information). **Actions triggered:** Ensuring compliance – e.g., a risk analyst will follow the DOA thresholds to route a large exposure for approval; an information analyst will apply the Information Security Classification Guideline to properly mark confidential data. If something isn’t covered by existing procedures, they may seek guidance from a manager or legal.

*   **Email and Chat Communications:** Analysts routinely receive emails or Teams messages from colleagues containing requests and information. **Sources:** A Relationship Manager might email an Information Analyst with context for a new customer to onboard, attaching documents. A credit underwriter might ping a risk analyst via Teams to ask if any new intel on a buyer has come up. Managers may forward policy updates or training material (e.g., a new job aid or Responsible AI one-pager). **What they look for:** Key details needed to do their work – deadlines, specific questions to answer, or new requirements. **Actions:** These communications initiate or adjust their tasks – e.g., an analyst will use the info to prioritize their queue or incorporate feedback into a report. They also often involve clarifying questions in return.

*   **Analytical Tools Outputs:** In some cases, analysts also “read” the output of automated tools or models – for example, outputs from the new **Analyst AI Agent** or a predictive model. **What they look for:** They interpret these outputs (like model-generated research summaries or risk scores) and scrutinize them for validity. **Actions:** If the output seems off, they will perform further investigation or ask the tool for assumptions. Useful outputs are then integrated into human-written reports or decisions, with sources or caveats noted.

## SECTION 6: COMMUNICATION AND STAKEHOLDERS

*   **Primary Internal Stakeholders:** Non-IT Analysts primarily serve *internal* stakeholders:
    *   **Front-line Business Teams:** Information Analysts support **Relationship Managers (RMs)** and **Account Managers** by providing correct customer data; analysts often liaise with RMs to clarify details about new customers or to update information. Risk Analysts partner closely with **Underwriters** and **Portfolio Managers** – underwriters are in the “first line” making credit decisions, but they rely on risk analysts for thorough buyer risk reviews and portfolio insights. Business Development and Market analysts support **Product Managers** and **Regional Sales Directors** with insights on market trends or client portfolios.
    *   **Functional Managers and Executives:** FP\&A and performance analysts serve the **Finance leadership (CFO and VPs)** and business unit heads by delivering financial analysis and forecasts. Their work often informs decision-making in management meetings. Similarly, risk analytics teams provide input to the **Chief Risk Officer (CRO)** and credit committees regarding portfolio risk and concentrations.
    *   **Supporting Departments:** Analysts interact with **IT and Data teams** when it comes to systems and data (for example, working with IT to fix a data feed or with Data Governance teams to improve a data definition). They also engage with **Legal/Compliance** on issues like confidentiality, sanctions, or policy interpretations (e.g., confirming if certain client info can be shared, or understanding new compliance procedures that could affect data handling).

*   **External Stakeholders:** Analysts’ roles are mostly internally facing, but there are exceptions:
    *   **Customers/Partners (Indirectly):** While analysts typically do not manage external relationships, the quality of their work directly impacts EDC’s external stakeholders. For instance, an Information Analyst’s diligence ensures that a customer’s information is correctly reflected in contracts and communications (avoiding misnaming a client company, for example, which could have legal implications if wrong). Risk Analysts occasionally may contact *external sources* for information – e.g., reaching out to a customer’s bank for a credit reference, or very rarely contacting the customer directly if needed for financial information (though standard practice is to obtain info via internal channels first).
    *   **Data Vendors and Research Providers:** Analysts also serve as the link to external data providers. They interact with account reps or interfaces of companies like S\&P (for CreditLens), Dun & Bradstreet, Moody’s, or data providers (Mergent, etc.) when pulling data. In some cases, they provide feedback to these vendors if data is missing or inconsistent. Economic analysts may engage with external economists or consultants for insights.
    *   **Auditors and Regulators:** On occasion, FP\&A or risk analysts might assist during audits or regulatory examinations by pulling data or explaining methodologies. For example, internal auditors or external auditors (e.g., Office of the Auditor General) may ask FP\&A analysts for details on how certain figures were derived. While those interactions are typically managed by managers, analysts prepare the data for such external scrutiny. In Risk, if EDC is asked by a regulator or during an OECD review about its portfolio, risk analysts help compile the needed analysis.

*   **Common Email Types:**
    *   **Data Requests and Responses:** A large share of analysts’ emails consist of responding to requests for information or analysis. Examples: *“Could you provide the latest numbers on \[X]?”* or *“Please find attached the credit analysis for \[Company Y]”*.
    *   **Clarifications and Collaboration:** Analysts also email back-and-forth internally to clarify data or inputs: e.g., an FP\&A analyst might email a product manager, *“Can you confirm the figure for Q4 bookings? It seems off from last month’s forecast”*, or a risk analyst emailing an RM, *“We noticed the customer’s name differs from the registration documents; can you verify the correct legal name?”*.
    *   **Update & Reminder Communications:** They receive plenty of broadcast communications relevant to their work: policy updates (like the rollout of the GenAI policy), new templates or job aids (e.g., an updated **BDO Dashboard access review** procedure involving FP\&A analysts), or notifications from systems (e.g., automated alerts from CRM or risk systems about data that needs attention).

*   **Meetings Led vs Attended:** Generally, non-IT analysts **attend and present in meetings more often than they lead them**:
    *   They **attend strategy and decision meetings as experts.** For instance, a risk analyst or economist might present at a risk forum or strategy offsite, sharing insights but not necessarily chairing the meeting.
    *   They may **lead smaller working sessions or training**. A senior Information Analyst could lead a Lunch & Learn on data best practices, or a Lead FP\&A Analyst might host a workshop for business managers on how to interpret a new dashboard. In the **CIM team**, senior analysts have led sessions on career growth and skills development.
    *   Within their own teams, an experienced analyst might lead internal huddles or project meetings. For example, a Lead Analyst in FP\&A could run a weekly checkpoint meeting with other analysts to coordinate the production of the monthly performance report.

*   **Presentations/Briefings:** Analysts regularly transform data into presentations or briefings:
    *   **Financial and Performance Briefings:** Provided to senior management on a set cadence (monthly, quarterly). FP\&A analysts present these, explaining financial results, KPIs, and variance drivers to VPs and the CFO. These briefings can be in executive committees or departmental meetings.
    *   **Risk Portfolio Reviews:** Risk analysts may brief credit managers or the Risk Committee on portfolio health – e.g., highlighting trends in credit quality, big exposures, or concentrations. They prepare charts and narratives on portfolio metrics like loss rates, sector concentrations, and watch-list accounts.
    *   **Customer/Deal Reviews:** Business development or credit analysts join major account review meetings, where they provide the analytical perspective (e.g., presenting a deep-dive on a customer’s creditworthiness or profitability ahead of a deal negotiation).
    *   **Frequency:** Formal briefings are often **monthly or quarterly**, aligned with reporting cycles. Ad-hoc presentations occur for specific initiatives or if emerging issues require analysis (e.g., a one-time briefing on the impact of a geopolitical event on EDC’s portfolio).

*   **Time-Intensive Communication Tasks:** A notable portion of an analyst’s communication effort goes into **translating complex data into digestible insights**:
    *   **Drafting Commentary:** Writing clear explanations for numerical results – e.g., an FP\&A analyst spends time crafting the narrative in a performance report to explain *why* a certain target was missed or exceeded. This requires communicating with context in plain language (avoiding overly technical terms) as per EDC’s preference for plain language.
    *   **Stakeholder Consultations:** Before finalizing an analysis, analysts often consult multiple stakeholders via meetings or email. For example, a risk analyst might discuss a tentative risk rating with a senior underwriter or gather input from a regional manager about a customer’s background. These discussions ensure the analysis is well-informed but can be time-consuming to coordinate.
    *   **Responding to Questions:** After delivering reports or analyses, analysts field follow-up questions. An FP\&A analyst might spend considerable time answering “drill-down” questions from a VP (e.g., *“Can you break down this expense increase by program?”*), often requiring a quick turn-around with additional data. A risk analyst might respond to underwriter queries on specific details of a buyer’s financials.
    *   **Meetings & Calls:** As mentioned, analysts attend many meetings – preparing for these (creating talking points or backup data) and then following up with minutes or additional info is another communication-heavy task.

*Overall, strong communication skills are essential: these roles act as **bridges between raw data and decision-makers**, meaning they must constantly tailor information for different audiences (from front-line account reps to the C-suite). This is a valued part of the analyst’s job but does require significant time and effort to do effectively.*

## SECTION 7: DATA AND INFORMATION HANDLING

*   **Types of Data Accessed:** Non-IT Analysts work with a broad spectrum of data. This includes **customer and counterparty information** (company names, addresses, ownership details, contacts); **financial statement data** (balance sheets, income statements, cash flows of clients or EDC’s own financials); **credit exposure and risk ratings**; **transaction details** (loans, insurance policies, amounts, dates); and various **internal metrics** (sales figures, portfolio performance indicators, operational metrics). They also use **macroeconomic and market data** (GDP figures, commodity prices, industry trends) for context in analysis. In some roles, analysts handle large datasets such as survey results (e.g., the economics team working with thousands of survey responses).

*   **Sensitive/Confidential Data:** Analysts frequently encounter **non-public and sensitive information.** This includes:
    *   **Personally Identifiable Information (PII):** e.g., contact details of company executives, beneficial owners’ identities, and in some cases financial details of individuals (for KYC, analysts might see passport or SIN numbers in compliance reports).
    *   **Confidential Business Information:** Clients’ financial statements (often private company financials not publicly available), business plans, and contract details. EDC treats client financials and business plans as confidential – analysts must protect these under confidentiality agreements and EDC’s **Code of Conduct** (which mandates proper handling of client info and avoiding unauthorized sharing).
    *   **Internal Financial/Strategic Data:** FP\&A analysts work with EDC’s own financial results before public release – considered sensitive until officially published. They also see strategic plans, budget assumptions, and risk appetite limits, which are confidential internally.
    *   **Risk Ratings and Credit Assessments:** These are sensitive both to EDC and the customer; they often include candid assessments of a company’s health. Such information is usually classified (e.g., “Protected B” or internal use only) under EDC’s **Information Security Classification Guideline**.

*   **Compliance and Regulatory Constraints:** Analysts must adhere to various regulatory and policy requirements:
    *   **Privacy Laws:** As a Canadian Crown corporation, EDC is subject to privacy legislation. Analysts must ensure that personal data (of customers or employees) is collected and used in compliance with the Privacy Act or PIPEDA (as applicable). For example, when handling personal identifiers of company owners or contacts, analysts only use them for authorized purposes and secure them properly.
    *   **Financial Crimes and Sanctions Compliance:** Risk and KYC analysts consider laws like the **Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act**, sanctions regulations, anti-bribery laws, etc. They must flag any potential issues (e.g., a customer in a sanctioned country or involved in prohibited sectors) during onboarding or credit analysis. EDC’s compliance protocols require analysts to involve the Financial Crimes compliance team if red flags arise, and systems like Finscan assist in this screening.
    *   **Export Development Act & Mandate Considerations:** EDC’s transactions must align with its mandate. Analysts in certain roles (e.g., those supporting the Investments team or new products) need to be aware of the **Export Development Act** limitations and ensure any proposed deal meets EDC’s public policy and mandate criteria.
    *   **Records and Information Management:** Analysts must follow EDC’s policies on information retention and classification. For instance, they should use approved repositories (e.g., SharePoint, CRM) for record-keeping rather than personal drives, and classify documents according to sensitivity. EDC’s **Data Governance and Management Standard** and Information Management policies guide these practices.
    *   **Responsible AI Use Constraints:** If analysts use Generative AI (e.g., Copilot or any AI tool) in their work, they are bound by EDC’s *Responsible Use of GenAI Policy*. **This policy explicitly forbids inputting any **Non-Public EDC information** or confidential data into external AI tools**. Analysts must ensure *no customer confidential data, financial results, or insider information is fed into AI prompts*. They also have to avoid using unapproved AI apps (only EDC-approved AI tools via company accounts are allowed).
    *   **Human Review and Transparency (AI Use):** When analysts do leverage AI (e.g., using M365 Copilot or an internal “Analyst Agent”), they **must thoroughly review and verify any AI-generated content** before using it in official work. EDC’s policy requires clearly labeling AI-generated content and not releasing it externally without proper human vetting. In practice, this means an analyst might use Copilot to draft a section of a report, but they need to fact-check every number and statement, and then note the sources, maintaining accountability.

*   **Financial Data Handled:** Analysts handle both **customer financial data** (revenues, profits, debt, etc. of client companies) and EDC’s own financial data. They regularly work with figures in the millions or billions of dollars (loan disbursements, insurance liabilities, revenue from premiums, etc.), and their analyses might influence **capital allocation and risk acceptance** decisions. Misstating these figures can have regulatory and financial implications. Thus, there are controls like peer reviews and system validations to reduce errors.

*   **PII Exposure:** Personal Identifiable Information exposure is moderate and mostly related to business contact info. Analysts in onboarding/KYC roles may see PII such as personal addresses, identification numbers, or ownership details of individuals (e.g., a company’s beneficial owners) when performing due diligence. All employees are reminded of their duty under EDC’s Confidential Information and Privacy policies to protect such data. PII and any customer-specific data is generally classified and should be stored only in approved systems (CRM, secure SharePoint libraries) and **never in public tools or personal devices**.

*   **Biggest Data-Related Risks:** The most critical risks include **inaccurate data leading to bad decisions** (e.g., incorrect financial data could result in approving a credit that defaults, or an error in a model could misguide strategic decisions). Another risk is **data leakage or misuse** – given the sensitivity of client and EDC data, an improper disclosure (even accidental, such as emailing a client’s financial statement to the wrong address) could breach confidentiality. Analysts also must guard against biases in data analysis – for example, ensuring a dataset is complete and not misrepresenting a trend. They rely on the **Info Security Classification and Data Governance guidelines** to determine handling of sensitive info. Additionally, in an AI context, there’s a risk of **over-reliance on AI without verification**; hence the emphasis on human oversight to catch AI “hallucinations” or errors that could propagate flawed data or insights.

*   **“Safe AI Use” Boundaries (EDC Policy):** Under EDC’s Responsible GenAI Policy, analysts (like all employees) must adhere to strict rules when using AI:
    *   *No sensitive data in public AI:* **Never input confidential or non-public EDC information into generative AI tools that are external or unapproved**. This includes customer data, internal financials, deal details, or any information not already public.
    *   *Use only approved platforms with corp credentials:* If using tools like MS Copilot or internal AI agents, they must log in with EDC accounts to leverage built-in data protections.
    *   *Mandatory human verification:* **AI outputs are advisory only** – analysts are required to review logic, check calculations, and confirm the accuracy of any AI-generated result before using it. For example, an analyst will spot-check figures that Copilot produces from a spreadsheet and ensure consistency.
    *   *Transparency:* Any content that does get used from AI must be attributed. For instance, if an analyst uses an AI to draft an email or report section, EDC expects them to clearly disclose that AI assistance was used and to ensure source references are included.
    *   *No autonomous decision-making:* The analyst (human) remains responsible for the final decision or recommendation. The **“Analyst Agent Guardrails”** explicitly state that humans are fully accountable – AI should not override professional judgment or be the final arbiter of decisions.

## SECTION 8: DECISIONS AND JUDGMENT

*   **Key Decisions Made by Analysts:** While many analyst roles are advisory (providing input for others’ decisions), there are decisions within their purview:
    *   **Data Acceptance & Classification:** Information Analysts decide when a company’s information is sufficiently verified to enter into the system. They might determine if two records represent the same entity and decide whether to merge them – a decision requiring caution to avoid mistaken identity. They also classify information (e.g., marking a document as “EDC Confidential”) which affects how it’s handled.
    *   **Risk Assessments & Escalation:** Risk Analysts decide on preliminary risk ratings and whether a credit file can be handled at their level or must be escalated. For example, they decide if a buyer’s new info is “Sensitive Risk” requiring RMO involvement or if it can be managed under pre-approved conditions. They also decide on recommendations (approve, decline, or require conditions) within their authority limits.
    *   **Analytical Approaches:** All analysts constantly decide **how to approach a problem** – what data to use, which assumptions to make, which methodology or model to apply. These methodological decisions can significantly affect outcomes (e.g., choosing a conservative vs aggressive scenario in a financial forecast, or deciding which valuation model to use for a credit). Good judgment here means choosing appropriate, justified approaches rather than arbitrary ones.
    *   **Prioritization Decisions:** With many incoming requests and tasks, analysts decide which items to tackle first. For instance, an analyst in a service role (like CIM or FP\&A) might triage tasks based on deadlines or importance – deciding that an urgent request from the CFO takes precedence over routine data updates.

*   **Where Mistakes Have the Biggest Consequences:**
    *   **Credit/Risk Errors:** A misjudgment by a risk analyst – such as underestimating a buyer’s credit risk – could lead to approving a large exposure that defaults, directly impacting EDC’s financial performance and potentially breaching risk appetite. Conversely, too conservative a stance could mean lost business. Mistakes in compliance-related checks (e.g., overlooking a sanctions hit or politically exposed person) could result in **legal/regulatory violations.** These high stakes mean risk analysts’ work is often double-checked by peers or managers for critical cases.
    *   **Data Quality Mistakes:** Errors by information analysts (like merging two distinct companies by mistake or inputting wrong legal names) can propagate through the organization – contracts might be issued with incorrect names, or exposure reports might aggregate customers improperly. Such mistakes can cause legal complications or customer dissatisfaction. EDC’s practice is that *“the lead officer must ensure all information relevant to the credit decision is documented in the file”*, underscoring the importance of complete and correct data.
    *   **Financial Analysis Mistakes:** An FP\&A analyst’s error in a financial model or report (e.g., a typo adding an extra zero, or a formula error) might misinform executives. While these reports are internal, they guide decisions on budgets, capital allocation, and strategy. A significant error could lead to misallocation of resources or loss of credibility of the Finance team. That’s why there are usually review processes – top performers are known to have robust checks to catch such errors before reports go out.

*   **Verification Before Action:** Analysts are expected to verify critical facts and calculations **before finalizing their work**:
    *   **Cross-Verification of Data:** For example, an information analyst must cross-check a company’s legal name against official registries **each time** – never relying solely on what a salesperson provided, because even small spelling errors can be problematic. They also run searches to ensure the company isn’t already in the system (to avoid duplicates).
    *   **Financial Calculations:** Risk analysts often use independent verification methods – e.g., manually recalculating key ratios or using a second spreadsheet to verify that the credit system’s output matches their expectations. They also check that data like aggregate group exposure limits in the system align with their own calculations and that any automated rating makes sense in context.
    *   **Sanity Checks:** All analysts apply a “reasonableness” test to results. They ask, *“Do these results make sense given what I know about the business context?”* If an FP\&A analyst’s model projects an unrealistically high growth in one product line, they will seek to understand why and perhaps adjust assumptions. This practice was formally captured in the *Analyst Agent Guardrails* as *“Business context sanity check – ask whether results align with known trends and operational reality; treat ‘technically correct but surprising’ results as risk signals”*.
    *   **Peer or Manager Reviews:** Before a major decision or report goes out, it’s common for analysts to have a colleague or superior review it. For instance, a risk analyst’s buyer assessment might be reviewed by a senior risk manager or another analyst to verify nothing was missed, especially for large deals.

*   **Escalation Triggers:** Analysts know when to pull in higher authorities:
    *   **Credit Exposure Thresholds:** EDC has clear thresholds when a credit decision must move up the chain. Risk Analysts and Underwriters follow the **Delegation of Authority (DOA)** matrix – e.g., if a buyer exposure exceeds a certain amount or risk level, the analyst must escalate to the RMO (Risk Management Office) or even the Board for approval. Missing these triggers is not acceptable – it’s a fundamental part of the job to recognize when a case needs higher sign-off.
    *   **Policy/Process Deviations:** If an analyst encounters a situation not covered by standard procedure (a “grey area”), they escalate to their manager or a relevant committee. For example, a new type of transaction that doesn’t fit existing credit guidelines would be brought to a policy advisor or a manager rather than the analyst deciding alone.
    *   **Data Anomalies or Conflicts:** Information Analysts will escalate if they find a serious data discrepancy – e.g., if a customer provided information that conflicts with an official source in a way that suggests potential fraud or risk, they alert Compliance or their manager. Similarly, if analysts in any role find errors in data that could compromise a decision (for instance, a suspected mistake in a model’s output that could affect reported financials), they raise it rather than silently proceed.
    *   **Workload or Deadline Risks:** An often overlooked but important judgment is knowing when to ask for help. If an analyst has too many high-priority tasks to meet all SLAs, a good practice is to inform management to redistribute work or adjust deadlines. EDC’s culture encourages escalating such capacity issues early, rather than letting something become overdue without visibility.

*   **What “Good Judgment” Looks Like:** In practical terms, analysts who demonstrate strong judgment:
    *   **Thoroughness with Pragmatism:** They ensure accuracy but also know when further precision has diminishing returns. For example, a high-performing FP\&A analyst will sense which variances in a financial report are material and focus on those, rather than getting lost in immaterial details. They apply the “80/20 rule” where appropriate, as highlighted in an Agile Analyst role description – being **adaptive in analysis as priorities shift and comfortable with 80/20 approaches**.
    *   **Reliance on Multiple Sources:** They rarely rely on a single source. A good risk analyst will verify a critical piece of information (like a customer’s debt levels) through multiple documents or databases, or confirm with the Info Sourcing team, rather than taking one report at face value.
    *   **Ethical and Confidential:** They show discretion – e.g., not sharing sensitive data loosely – and uphold EDC’s ethical standards in analysis (no bias or manipulation of numbers). They also challenge data or instructions that seem unethical (for instance, if asked to “fudge” a number, a good analyst would refuse and escalate concerns).
    *   **Proactive Risk Identification:** In credit and compliance contexts, good judgment means anticipating potential issues (e.g., spotting early warning signs in a customer’s financial health or compliance red flags) and taking preventive action. It also means knowing when an AI or model’s result seems suspect and needs deeper digging rather than blind acceptance.
    *   **Clear Communication:** They don’t bury bad news. If analysis reveals something concerning (e.g., a project is not financially viable, or a portfolio is over-concentrated in one risk), a strong analyst communicates this clearly with evidence and doesn’t hide the finding. They provide decision-makers with an honest assessment, even if the message is unwelcome, and suggest constructive solutions or alternatives.

## SECTION 9: PAIN POINTS AND TIME SINKS

*   **Common Frustrations (Evidence-Based):**
    *   **Data Accessibility & Tools:** Analysts in research and analytics roles often struggle with *data access and storage limitations*. One documented pain point from EDC’s Economics team is the **lack of a proper data environment**, forcing “quantitative analysts” to store large datasets on network drives and manually move data into Excel and Python for analysis – a *“highly inefficient”* process. Similarly, analysts might find it frustrating that some needed data (e.g., customer info, risk data, financial data) resides in siloed systems that don’t talk to each other, requiring repetitive manual extraction and reconciliation.
    *   **Manual Data Entry:** Despite working with numbers, analysts often find themselves performing manual work that could be automated. Before the introduction of new tools, *financial spreading* was a manual affair – a “spreader” had to key in all figures from PDF financial statements into the system. This repetitive task is both time-consuming and prone to error, and is recognized as a prime candidate for automation (hence the initiative to use AI for auto-spreading).
    *   **Multiple Systems & Re-Keying:** Many processes require using several systems in tandem. For instance, a risk analyst might need to update data in CRM, then in CIS for insurance, and also in a separate Excel sheet, because the systems aren’t fully integrated. This double or triple entry is a common gripe.
    *   **Information Bottlenecks:** Analysts sometimes face delays waiting for information from others. A credit analyst might be stuck until a Relationship Manager provides additional financial info, or an FP\&A analyst might wait on another department to send input for the forecast. These dependencies can cause stress, especially when up against a deadline.
    *   **Legacy Processes:** Some analysts mention that processes haven’t caught up with new technology. For example, printing and manually signing documents for approvals (when digital approvals could suffice) or maintaining physical files in parallel to electronic ones can feel redundant. The need to follow strict protocols (while necessary for compliance) sometimes feels bureaucratic – e.g., filling out lengthy standard forms for relatively small or routine analyses.

*   **Candidates for Automation/Acceleration:** Based on frustrations:
    *   **Financial Spreading:** High on the list. The use of AI (like the **QuiQspread** tool) to automate capturing financial statement data has been prioritized. This will free analysts from typing numbers and let them focus on interpreting results.
    *   **Data Cleansing and Merging:** Information analysts handle large volumes of data updates that could be accelerated with automation. For example, identifying duplicate company records or updating addresses could be aided by automated matching algorithms – reducing the manual search in multiple places.
    *   **Reporting & Slide Generation:** Preparing the same slides every month could be streamlined. There’s interest in using Copilot to draft repetitive narrative or even auto-generate PowerPoint slides from updated data. This is seen as a way to give time back to analysts for deeper analysis.
    *   **Routine Monitoring:** Creating alerts and dashboards to automatically flag outliers (e.g., a covenant breach, or a sales KPI drop) is another area. Instead of manually checking multiple reports, analysts could set up triggers so they only act when thresholds are crossed. EDC’s PPI team has been working on automation solutions like bots for credit limit inputs and other routine tasks to save thousands of hours.

*   **Bottlenecks in Workflows:**
    *   **Handoffs and Approvals:** These are necessary for checks and balances but can slow things down. For credit analysts, a bottleneck is waiting for RMO or Board approval for large deals – the time between completing an analysis and the final decision can be lengthy, and they often have to respond to subsequent questions in the interim.
    *   **Info Gathering from Clients:** Another bottleneck is external – if a client delays sending financial statements or answers for KYC questions, the analysis cannot proceed. Analysts often have little control over these timelines.

## SECTION 12: REGULATORY AND COMPLIANCE CONTEXT

*   **Relevant Regulations and Standards:** As a financial Crown corporation, many of EDC’s analyst activities are framed by regulatory requirements:
    *   **Export Development Act & Corporate Policies:** EDC’s operations are governed by the Export Development Act and associated regulations. Analysts, particularly those in underwriting, must ensure transactions align with EDC’s mandate and any limitations (e.g., certain types of business the corporation can or cannot support). EDC’s internal policies (e.g., Mandate Policy, Delegation of Authority Standards) codify these rules, and analysts need to be familiar with them when structuring or reviewing deals.
    *   **Financial Regulations:** EDC must adhere to financial regulations similar to banks in areas such as anti-money laundering (AML) and know-your-customer (KYC). Analysts in onboarding and risk roles incorporate compliance checks for:
        *   **Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act (PCMLTFA):** ensuring no facilitation of money laundering or terrorist financing – e.g., verifying customers against sanctions lists and high-risk country lists.
        *   **Sanctions (UN, Canadian sanctions laws):** verifying that EDC does not support transactions in sanctioned countries or with sanctioned parties. Analysts rely on tools like Finscan to catch these, but must interpret and escalate hits properly.
        *   **Environmental and Social Review:** Though typically a specialized team handles E\&S risk, analysts (especially in project finance or certain insurance deals) might need to ensure transactions go through the environmental risk review process if required by EDC policy or Canadian law.
        *   **Data Privacy (Privacy Act/PIPEDA):** Analysts must only use personal data as allowed and ensure it’s protected. This also impacts how data is shared in analysis – for example, if using case stories or customer examples in a presentation, they must anonymize or aggregate to avoid exposing personal information.
    *   **Public Disclosure and Transparency:** As a corporation with public accountability, EDC has a **Transparency and Disclosure Policy**. Analysts contributing to anything that may eventually be publicly disclosed (like aggregated portfolio info in annual reports or on the website) need to ensure data is accurate and appropriately scrubbed of confidential details. For example, External Communications provides protocols for how to handle media inquiries – while not directly the analyst’s job, if an analyst is ever looped in to provide data for a public press release, they must abide by approved figures and messaging.

*   **Client Communication Rules & Record-Keeping:** EDC has standards for communications with customers and recording those interactions:
    *   **Media and Public Statements:** If an analyst, in rare cases, is contacted by external parties (e.g., asked to comment on an economic trend), they must follow the **EDC Media Protocol** – typically referring any media queries to the External Communications team.
    *   **Documentation retention:** All analyses that support decisions (credit memos, spreadsheets supporting ratings, etc.) must be saved in official systems so they can be retrieved for audits or reviews. EDC’s internal control guidelines emphasize documenting assumptions and maintaining records for decisions as part of the control framework. Analysts are responsible for ensuring their analysis files are stored appropriately (e.g., on SharePoint or in the transaction’s electronic folder).
    *   **Audit Trails:** There’s an expectation of auditability. For example, the credit file must contain the rationale for decisions, and any deviations from standard responsibilities need manager approval and documentation. Analysts might be called upon during audits to explain or show evidence of their analysis process.

*   **Consequences of Compliance Failures:** While specific internal cases aren’t documented here, generally:
    *   Failing to comply with credit policy (e.g., granting a credit without proper approval) can result in *financial losses and internal disciplinary action*. It also undermines the risk governance framework.
    *   Mishandling confidential information (e.g., sending customer financials through insecure channels or leaking non-public info via AI tools) could lead to *investigations under EDC’s Code of Conduct*, potential employment consequences, and reputational damage. The Code of Conduct and related standards apply to all employees and emphasize safeguarding confidential information and avoiding conflicts of interest.
    *   Misuse of AI tools (like inputting secret data) could lead to suspension of access to those tools and other corrective measures, given the strong stance EDC has on Responsible AI use.

*   **Restrictions on AI Use for Specific Tasks:** EDC’s Responsible AI Policy provides clear restrictions relevant to analysts:
    *   **No using GenAI for prohibited content or decisions:** Analysts cannot use AI to draft content that violates privacy or fairness norms (for instance, they shouldn’t use AI to write performance reviews or to process HR-sensitive data, as those contain personal information). They also cannot use AI to make decisions that should involve human judgment, such as approving a credit or changing a risk rating – AI might assist in analysis, but final decisions are human-driven.
    *   **No external sharing of data with AI providers:** Analysts must treat AI prompts as public – meaning any data typed into, say, ChatGPT, effectively leaves EDC’s control. Thus, they are restricted from using any non-public data in such prompts.
    *   **Maintaining transparency:** If an analyst were to use AI to assist in creating a document, the policy (and best practice) is that they should label and treat it as such. For example, pasting an entire client’s financial statement into an AI tool to get a summary is not allowed; instead, the analyst could extract generic insights with AI, but would need to manually verify and then document sources in the final output.

*Analysts operate under a strong culture of compliance. They often serve as the “guardians” of data integrity and process adherence, so they are expected to be models of following these regulations and policies in their day-to-day work. EDC’s training and guidelines (like the Data Governance playbook, Information Security guidelines, and GenAI policy) are there to support them in this aspect, and analysts are typically well-versed in these rules.*

## SECTION 13: AI TRAINING DESIGN SEEDS

### A) Operational Anchors (Real Tasks for AI Skills Exercises)

1.  **Data Cleanup & Deduplication:** Use an AI tool (e.g., Copilot in Excel) to identify potential duplicate company entries in the CRM export and suggest a merged record, testing *Tool Fluency* and *Data Safety* skills (ensuring no sensitive data is exposed and results are verified against official sources).

2.  **Credit Memo Drafting:** Leverage Copilot in Word to draft a *credit risk assessment* for a new customer using provided financial data. The learner must prompt the AI for an executive summary and key financial ratios, then apply *Verification and Judgment* by correcting any AI mistakes and confirming all facts before finalizing the memo.

3.  **Portfolio Dashboard Analysis:** Ask Copilot (integrated with Power BI) to generate insights from a portfolio performance dashboard (e.g., trends in signed business volume or NPS) for a monthly report. The learner practices *Prompting for Outcomes* (crafting effective queries) and *Tool Fluency* with Power BI, then interprets and verifies the AI’s analysis for accuracy before presenting it.

4.  **Email to Client Draft – Sensitive Data Handling:** Given a scenario with detailed client information, use an AI email assistant to draft a customer communication. The learner must demonstrate *Data Safety and Compliance* by only including sanitized, non-confidential information in the AI prompt and ensuring the draft contains no restricted details, in line with EDC’s GenAI policy.

5.  **Financial Spread QA:** Provide an AI (e.g., Excel formula assistant or Python script via Copilot) with a completed financial spreadsheet to identify any errors or outliers. The learner must show *Verification and Judgment* by double-checking the AI’s flagged items and confirming the financial calculations match the source data, thereby reinforcing that AI is used as a second pair of eyes, not an autonomous authority.

6.  **Meeting Prep Summarization:** Use Copilot’s meeting recap capabilities on a recorded Teams meeting where a lot of numbers and action items were discussed. The learner’s task is to obtain a summary via AI (*Prompting for Outcomes*) and then practice *Judgment* by comparing the summary to the actual transcript, ensuring no key details were missed or misrepresented before sharing the recap with the team.

7.  **Scenario Analysis with AI:** Present a set of economic indicators and have the learner prompt an AI tool (like a custom “Analyst” agent) to produce a quick scenario forecast for a given portfolio. They then evaluate the AI’s output, cross-check it with known trends, and adjust assumptions as needed (*Verification and Judgment*, *Tool Fluency* in using advanced AI features). The exercise tests the learner’s ability to guide an AI to useful outputs and critically appraise the results.

8.  **KPI Commentary Generation:** Provide the learner with a raw dataset of performance metrics (e.g., a CSV of KPIs vs targets) and ask them to use Copilot or another AI to draft a narrative highlights report. The learner must refine prompts to get a concise summary (*Prompting for Outcomes*), then use *Verification* to ensure all statements are backed by the data, and apply *Data Safety* by removing any reference to internal-only metrics that should not be widely circulated. This tests judgement in selecting what insights are suitable to share and how to accurately communicate them with AI assistance.
